**Reading the table**
- **Hand-coded rules** are rupee-exact on all 256 FY 2024-25 cases and all 238 FY 2025-26 cases, deterministic, about 0.3 ms per request, and every rupee traces to a clause (income lines sum to taxable income, tax lines sum to total tax).
- **LLM at query time** (Qwen3.5-9B, about 3,100 tokens per request) was exact on 3 of 12 cases. The three it got right were all plain slab-edge cases. It was wrong on every case involving a surcharge, the 87A rebate, deduction caps or a mix of deductions, and **none of those wrong answers raised an error** (9 silent, 0 loud). Its traces satisfy only 55% of the traceability checks. It was deterministic here (1 distinct output over 10 runs of 2 inputs) because the local server runs at temperature 0; I would not assume that of a hosted model. Latency about 20 s per request.
- **LLM-compiled rules**: after review the module is as exact and traceable as the hand-coded one and runs without an LLM (0.5 ms). The review is the cost: the raw model output was 0% exact (crashed on every case), and after only the crash was fixed it was silently wrong on 194 of 256 cases (case-exact 24%); 17 defects are listed in `generated/REVIEW.md`, including a surcharge ladder charging 10% on incomes above Rs 5 lakh, and invented section numbers.
- **Parser** (50 descriptions, 28 complete + 22 that need a question): the regex baseline extracts 25% of complete descriptions exactly and asks too many questions (19 of 28 unneeded); the LLM extractor with the same deterministic validation extracts 89% (25 of 28) exactly with 0 invented fields. With the final rules the LLM parser **guessed instead of asking in 3 of 22 cases** (d39: a same-message self-correction, "12 lakh, no wait, 15 lakh", that it resolved to 15 lakh, arguably acceptable; d46: it is unclear whether a bonus is included; d48: "I am 55 ... and I am under 40", a contradiction it missed, which the regex baseline missed too), asked **2 unneeded questions** (d26, d27) and made 1 wrong extraction (d15, an 80D amount). The regex baseline guessed in 2 of 22 (d38, d48) but asked an unneeded question on 19 of 28 complete descriptions, so it is safe only because it refuses to understand most text. Latency and cost for the LLM parser come from the uncached run of 50 live calls (p50 4.9 s, about $0.13 per 1,000 requests at Gemini Flash list price); the final accuracy figures were re-scored from cached model answers after the rule changes below.
- **Two flaws found by reading raw outputs, both fixed with rules and not by prompting alone** (so the 50 descriptions are now a development set). (1) The first LLM-parser run asked an unneeded question on 22 of 28 complete descriptions: the model labels the salary basis "unclear" whenever the text says just "salary", and my validation treated that as ambiguous; the fix was a rule (plain "salary" is read as gross; only an explicit CTC or take-home triggers a question). (2) The model **invented an age** (60) from "I'm a senior citizen" and **assumed monthly pay** for "I earn 90000". Fix: evidence guards in `validate()`: a claimed age must be written in the text (digits or number words), and "monthly" needs a month cue, otherwise the rule asks. Three generic prompt instructions were also tightened (taxpayer's own age only, report the gross figure when both CTC and gross are given, sum 80C items). A latent bug was also found in my regex baseline (word-boundary escapes had become backspace characters); fixing it changed none of its results, which confirms its weakness is real (for instance its "salary" cue window also catches the 80C amount that follows and sees two conflicting salaries).

Takeaways: (1) the hand-coded design absorbs the change as a data diff, and its only silent risk is a *partial* update, which is exactly what a test suite built around every threshold catches (both partial-update experiments fail 65 and 150 of the suite's cases); (2) the LLM-at-query-time design has no mechanism to make a change safe, because there is no test it can pass; (3) compiling gives rule-engine behaviour at run time but moves all the risk to a review that was long and found 17 defects, and a Budget change should never mean "regenerate the module".

## 4. Final design (Task 6)

**Architecture for 100,000 users. Each component is labelled and tied to a measurement above.**

| Component | Label | Why (measurement) |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding free text (Hinglish, Hindi, terse, messy) into a fixed JSON of facts | **LLM** | 89% exact on complete descriptions versus 25% for the regex baseline, 0 invented fields; about 4.9 s and $0.13 per 1,000 requests |
| Ask-or-proceed decision: required fields, contradictions, CTC / take-home / bare-number ambiguity, rent / loan / parent-insurance details, evidence guards, city -> metro, annualising, list of assumptions | **rule** | the model proposes, the rules decide: they stopped 2 of its guesses (invented age, assumed monthly pay) and unneeded questions fell from 22 to 2; they are why "guessed instead of asking" is 3 of 22 and not far worse |
| Tax computation: standard deduction, HRA, 80C / 80CCD(1B) / 80D, home-loan interest, slabs, 87A with marginal relief, surcharge with marginal relief, cess, Rs 10 rounding | **rule** (code + one YAML per FY) | rupee-exact on 494 of 494 cases, deterministic, 0.3 ms, about $0; the FY change was 26 YAML lines and 0 code lines |
| Regime recommendation and the "why" | **rule** (template filled from the computed figures) | arithmetic on two exact totals; an LLM here only adds risk |
| Audit trace (clause per rupee, lines sum to the totals) | **rule** | 100% of trace checks pass; the LLM-at-query-time output passes 55% |
| Handling each Budget | **hybrid: human + LLM assist** | see below |
| LLM computing the tax at query time | **not shipped** | 3 of 12 cases exact, 9 silent wrong answers and 0 errors, 20 s, about $0.54 per 1,000 at list price (about $54 per 100,000), 55% traceable |
| LLM-compiled rules as a pipeline | **not shipped** | raw draft 0% exact, then 24% after the crash fix (194 of 256 silently wrong), 17 defects including invented clauses; a recompile is a different program; after review it equals the hand-coded rules, so it buys nothing at run time and its risk sits in the review |

**Why this split.** A tax figure has to be rupee-exact, explainable clause by clause, identical on every call and cheap to change; only the rules meet all four, and rules cannot read "meri salary 14 lakh hai, kiraya 25000". So the LLM touches only the text, and the rules decide what to compute. The measured failure of the LLM designs is not that they are sometimes wrong but that they are wrong **silently**: no error, a plausible trace, a different rupee amount.

**Keeping it correct through the next Budget (the process matters more than the code).**
1. *Freeze and archive* the new rule text with clause ids. The LLM may **propose** the YAML diff, never regenerate code (the FY 2025-26 recompile crashed on 225 of 238 cases).
2. *Change numbers only* in a new `fyYYYY_YY.yaml`; change code only for a new kind of rule. An unknown FY already fails loudly.
3. *A second person updates the oracle independently* (not by copying the YAML). A disagreement between the two is the main detector; here both were written by one assistant, a known weakness.
4. *Boundary tests at every threshold of the new law* (+/-10 around each slab edge, the 87A limit and each surcharge band). Partial updates are the realistic hazard: updating the slabs but forgetting the 87A limit gave 65 silent failures, the reverse 150; a suite built around the thresholds fails loudly on both. Next step: derive the thresholds from the YAML in `build_suite.py` so a forgotten one is impossible.
5. *Release gate:* the full suite, at least 30 inputs checked against the Income Tax Department's official calculator including every boundary row, and a mutation check (change each constant by 1; at least one test must fail).
6. *After release:* every answer carries the FY and a hash of the YAML; a weekly canary compares 30 random inputs with the official calculator; a visible "this looks wrong" path sends the trace to support.

**Scale and cost at 100,000 users.** The calculator is stateless and CPU-only (about 3,000 requests per second per core), so it is not the constraint. The LLM is: about 5 s per free-text request and about $13 per 100,000 requests at Gemini Flash list price. Structured form entry should bypass the LLM entirely. One local 9B model on a laptop GPU handles about one request every 5 s, so production needs a hosted model or a pool with queueing; if the model is unavailable the service degrades to the regex extractor plus form fields, which asks more questions and never guesses. Salary data is personal, so use a provider with a data-processing agreement or a self-hosted model. **Known limits:** the LLM numbers come from one small local model and small samples; the official-calculator check still has to be done by a person; the compiled-rules review was done by the AI assistant.
